# Aomori Nebuta Festival 2026

## Overview

The **Nebuta Matsuri** (ねぶた祭り) is one of Japan's most famous and spectacular festivals, held annually in **Aomori City** in the Tohoku region of northern Japan. The festival draws over **3 million visitors** each year with its enormous illuminated floats, costumed dancers, and thunderous percussion. It has been designated an **Important Intangible Folk Cultural Property of Japan** (since 1980).

Nebuta is one of the **Tohoku Sandai Matsuri** — the Three Great Festivals of the Tohoku region — alongside the **Akita Kanto Matsuri** (August 3–6) and the **Sendai Tanabata Matsuri** (August 6–8). Festival travelers frequently combine all three in a single trip.

## The Floats (Nebuta)

- **20+ massive floats** parade through the streets each night, handcrafted by specialist artisans called **Nebutashi** (nebuta masters)
- Dimensions: up to **9 meters wide, 5 meters tall, and 7 meters deep**
- Weight: up to **4 tons** (including the float base)
- Construction: painted **washi (Japanese paper)** stretched over **wire-and-wood frames**, illuminated from inside
- Subjects: **warriors, mythological beings, kabuki actors, historical figures** — depicted in combat with snakes, skulls, and evil spirits, faces cast in fearsome expressions
- Each float is **rocked, spun, and raced** through the streets by pullers
- Floats grow larger and more elaborate each year; the Nebuta Festival grew significantly in scale during the **Meiji era (1868–1912)**

### How Floats Are Made

Nebuta masters begin planning new designs immediately after each year's festival concludes. The process:

1. **Winter** — First design draft completed
2. **Spring** — Detailed parts (face, arms, legs) sculpted first
3. **By May** — Full wire-and-wood structure built in studios
4. **Papering** — Light bulbs installed inside; structure covered with traditional washi on the inside
5. **Finishing** — Outlines drawn in black charcoal ink; patterns drawn with melted candles; final color applied with paintbrushes and spray

## The Haneto Dancers

Costumed dancers called **Haneto** precede and follow the floats, jumping and chanting **"Rassera, rassera! Rasse, rasse, rasse ra!"** Anyone dressed in the haneto costume may join — no advance booking required.

### Haneto Costume Components

| Item | Description |
|---|---|
| **Hanagasa** | Triangular straw hat with colorful artificial flowers |
| **Yukata** | Simple white-based kimono; hem rolled up to the knee |
| **Okoshi** | Pink or light blue undergarment worn beneath the yukata |
| **Shigoki** | Decorative waist band |
| **Gagashiko** | Tin container (for water or alcohol) |
| **Shoulder strap** | Bright colors — red, pink |
| **Tabi** | White split-toe socks |
| **Zori** | Traditional straw sandals; bind with *mameshibori* cloth to keep from slipping off |

**Rental cost:** ~¥4,000 for the basic set (tabi, zori, and hanagasa available for additional cost)  
Costumes can also be purchased at department stores in Aomori City.

## The Music (Nebuta Bayashi)

Nebuta bayashi bands set the rhythm for the entire procession:

| Instrument | Role |
|---|---|
| **Taiko** (大太鼓) | Large drums; set the driving rhythm |
| **Fue** (笛) | Flute; carries the melody |
| **Teburigane** (手振鐘) | Hand cymbals; accentuate the beat |

## Rassera Land (Nebuta Rasse Land)

Before and during the festival, visitors can see floats up close at **Rassera Land** — a venue of 22 builder's huts in **Aoi-umi Park**, next to the Aomori Prefectural Center for Tourism and Industry (ASPAM).

- **Address:** 2-1 Yasukata, Aomori City (by ASPAM)
- **Open:** May – August 7
- **Access:** 8-minute walk from JR Aomori Station
- Visitors can meet the nebuta designers and see floats being constructed or displayed during the day

## Origins

The name *nebuta* (or *neputa*) is believed to derive from **nemuta** ("sleepiness"). In ancient times, farmers in the busy summer season would float paper lanterns down rivers to symbolically carry their sleepiness away — a practice originally associated with the **Tanabata star festival**. Over centuries, this ritual merged with Obon ancestor-spirit observances and local Tsugaru customs, gradually evolving into the grand street festival known today.

The floats grew progressively larger through the **Meiji era**. Traditionally created by local neighborhood councils, the production of nebutas has increasingly shifted to businesses and organizations. The festival is still technically considered a **fire festival**, recalling its origins when floats were lit by candles.

## Nearby: Wa Rasse Museum (Nebuta House)

The **Nebuta no Ie Wa Rasse** is a year-round museum dedicated to the festival, located near Aomori Station. It displays full-scale nebuta floats from previous years and explains their construction and history — an excellent option if your schedule doesn't align with the August dates, or as a daytime activity during festival week before the evening parade.

---

## Related: Goshogawara Tachineputa (Day Trip Option)

**Goshogawara** (立佞武多, *Tachineputa*), about 75 minutes by train from Aomori, holds its own neputa festival in early August featuring dramatically tall **standing floats** — some reaching over 20 meters high — a striking contrast to Aomori's wider, lower nebutas. Feasible as a day trip from Aomori; the last train back to Aomori typically runs around 21:30 during festival nights.

Other nearby neputa festivals in the same week:
- **Hirosaki Neputa Matsuri** — August 1–7, Hirosaki City (~1 hr from Aomori)
- **Kuroishi Neputa Festival** — late July to early August

## Getting There & Logistics

- **From Tokyo:** Tohoku Shinkansen to Aomori Station (approx. 3 hours)
- **From Aomori Airport:** ~30 minutes by car
- **From Aomori Chuo IC:** ~15 minutes by car
- **Parade access:** 10-minute walk from JR Aomori Station

> **Book accommodation very early.** Hotels in Aomori City fill up months in advance. Last-minute lodging during festival week is essentially unavailable.

> **Train frequency warning.** Tohoku train services outside major hubs often run on hourly schedules. Missing a train — especially on the return from a day trip like Goshogawara — can mean a 30–60 minute delay. Always check the last train time before heading out.
